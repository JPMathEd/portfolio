Introduction

I'm Joshua Plummer, a Lecturer in Mathematics at Auburn University at Montgomery and an Ed.D. student in Instructional Systems and Learning Sciences, with a concentration in Instructional Technology. My work connects mathematics teaching, active learning, and accessible instructional design.

In INST 7800, I examined AI tools and their educational uses, practiced structured prompting, and considered ethical questions about learning and judgment. I then connected learning theory to an instructional design, tested its behavior, audited equity and accessibility, and planned how I might lead responsible adoption. My final capstone, the AI Error Coach, brings that work together: a question-based coach that helps students find where their reasoning broke after a test.

The artifacts below follow module order. Modules 5 through 8 come together in my final capstone, at the end of this page. Together, they show how my attention shifted from producing useful materials more quickly to asking what students learn, who keeps the judgment, and who a design leaves out.

Module 2 · Tool evaluation

Review of NotebookLM

I evaluated NotebookLM using the SECTIONS framework and learning-theory perspectives, focusing on learner agency, review, interaction, and the quality of source materials. I recommended pairing instructor-curated review with a task that asks students to explain, apply, or extend an idea so that a polished summary does not stand in for learning.

Module 3 · Professional development

Prompt Engineering PD

I adapted Park and Choo's IDEA framework into a facilitator guide with a 45–60-minute workshop and a longer three-hour format. The guide emphasizes testing prompts, documenting revisions, checking outputs, and checking whether participants use what they learned in their teaching. The included rubric and diagnostic guidance help facilitators identify where participants need support and decide how to adjust the session.

Module 4 · Ethical analysis

Ethical AI Use

I examined the tension between authentic learning and fairness when an AI detector flags a student's work. My response argues for conversation about the student's reasoning, transparent attribution, and teaching that adapts to AI use while retaining human judgment.

Reflections

Summarized from Section 7 of my final capstone, “My AI Leadership Philosophy.”

From saving time to designing for learning

I began the course mostly seeing AI as a way to reduce routine work. That still matters, but learning theory helped me see a more purposeful role: shaping an interaction around how students learn from their own mistakes. The Error Coach became my clearest example of that shift.

Keep judgment with people

I now begin an AI decision by asking whether it helps students learn, who retains judgment, and who it leaves out. My audit made the third question concrete. A tool can be available to everyone and still provide different levels of support, so students need a way to complete the same work without AI.

Let evidence set the pace

I want to experiment freely in my own work while moving more deliberately when students or colleagues are affected. Version logs and phased implementation help me connect a change to the problem it is meant to solve. I am continuing to develop my collaboration and coaching skills. The workshop gives me a way to practice both with colleagues.

Check the thinking partner

I used AI to organize ideas, question conclusions, and edit this capstone. I also had to catch it returning to discarded designs and shortening sentences until their meaning blurred. The habit I want to model is checking AI against the source and treating its judgment as one input while keeping responsibility for the work.

Final Capstone Project

Part 2: Final Capstone

Post-Test Error Diagnosis: A Hybrid AI–Human Instructional Model for Mathematics

I designed a six-step process in which students examine a missed problem with an AI coach or in a short meeting with me. The capstone brings together the design, learning theory, eight test rounds, a twelve-session simulated equity audit, and a proposed leadership plan.

Current prompt card v2.2 incorporates the audit's recommendations but has not been tested. Students are using an early version in fall 2026; student learning outcomes have not been measured, and the comparison study remains a proposal.
