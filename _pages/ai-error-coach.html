AI Error Coach.

Post-Test Error Diagnosis: A Hybrid AI–Human Instructional Model for Mathematics

I designed a guided reflection process that helps students examine their own reasoning after a graded test. Students choose an AI coach or a short meeting with me, and the thinking stays with the student.

The design challenge

What happens after the test?

Students often get a graded mathematics test back, notice what they missed, and move on. The reasoning that produced the mistake can go unexamined and follow them into the next topic.

I wanted students to identify where their thinking broke, explain the cause, and decide what to do differently next time. Individual questioning can support that work, but I cannot guide every student through every missed problem at once. The Error Coach is my attempt to extend that questioning while keeping assessment and instructional judgment with me.

How it works

One problem. Six thinking steps.

A student chooses one problem from a graded, returned test. On the AI route, they paste prompt card v2.2 into a new free ChatGPT conversation. The card asks the coach to check how the student wants mathematics presented—symbols, words, or both—and read the problem and original work back before coaching.

  1. Explain the original thinking. The student describes what they were trying to do.
  2. Find where it broke. They identify the first point where their reasoning went off track.
  3. Name the error type. They choose a category and explain why it fits.
  4. Repair the reasoning. They rework the problem and justify each changed line.
  5. Write a cue for next time. They connect something they can recognize to an action they can take.
  6. Transfer the idea. They build a new problem from their own life. When a real-life setting does not fit, they change one feature of their original problem.

The card prohibits grading, giving or confirming answers, solving the problem, and repeating identifiable information. Hints come in stages and only after an attempt. If the student remains stuck, the coach is instructed to stop and flag the problem for me.

The meeting route is an equal choice.

Any student can work through the same six steps and help ladder with me in a short meeting, in person or by video. We use the six-step sheet as our agenda. In person, this route requires no AI account, device, or data plan.

Every student submits their own Error Analysis and reflection. Students who choose the chat also provide screenshots; students who choose a meeting provide the six-step sheet from our meeting. I grade the student's reasoning; the coach does not assign the grade.

What I assess

Appendix C of the capstone sets out the 10-point Error Analysis rubric that students see before they begin:

  • Original thinking and diagnosis — 2 points: Explain the original reasoning, locate its first real break, and justify the error classification.
  • Justified rework — 3 points: Rework the problem and explain each step.
  • Independent Error Analysis — 2 points: Write the analysis independently, in the student's own words, after coaching ends.
  • Cue, action, and transfer — 2 points: Write a specific plan for next time and explain how the idea applies in another situation.
  • Complete evidence — 1 point: Submit the original work, rework, and Error Analysis with either the chat screenshots or the meeting sheet.

The rubric grades the student's reasoning. Screenshots document the conversation and card version, but they cannot prove how independently a student worked.

Why this design

Keep the learner doing the reasoning.

I built the routine around four behaviorist principles from Ormrod (2011): feedback connected to the student's response, help following an attempt, attention to what caused an error, and cues that support a different response next time.

Universal Design for Learning adds choices across the process. Students can choose a chat or a meeting, request mathematics in symbols or words, and develop a transfer problem from a setting that matters to them. These choices are part of the design from the beginning.

Those principles also set limits. Credit depends on the student's reasoning rather than simply finishing a conversation. Keeping the routine to one problem helps make the reflection manageable.

Testing & revision

Each version has its own evidence.

v2.0: Eight test rounds

I tested the coach in eight rounds and evaluated it with the DOMS rubric. The record exposed problems with mathematical reasoning, the help ladder, and the boundary between questioning and supplying a diagnosis. Those tests informed sixteen changes in v2.1.

DOMS provided a structure for my judgments. Its scores are not a validated measure of student learning.

v2.1: Twelve audit sessions

I ran the equity audit myself, simulating different student behaviors. I examined language and cultural contexts, requests for help, access to different models, and how mathematical notation sounded through screen readers.

These were my test interactions, not a study with twelve students. I used a script and translation for the Spanish session, and I did not test phones or connection speed.

What the equity audit revealed

The coach treated names, Spanish, and casual writing with respect in the tested interactions. That did not mean every interaction provided equally useful coaching. One Spanish session raised a question about the category step, but the evidence could not separate a language effect from ordinary variation between runs.

  • Pressure changed the help. Students who pushed harder could receive stronger hints or reopen a session that should have stopped.
  • Access changed the coaching. In my comparison, the free model provided weaker coaching than the paid model. This was a finding about the models and sessions I tested.
  • Mathematics was lost in audio. Screen readers skipped or garbled parts of the notation, and useful plain-text explanations appeared only after a request.

v2.2: The current, untested revision

I carried three recommendations into v2.2: describe coaching moves instead of supplying scripts to copy, tie help to attempts rather than pressure, and let every student choose how mathematics is presented. The card also checks ambiguous read-backs and asks students to consult their notes or textbook before the coach stops.

These changes need retesting. The longer card may itself be harder for a model to follow.

The leadership plan

Build capacity, then decide what comes next.

I designed a 60-minute workshop around UNESCO's AI Competency Framework for Teachers. Colleagues would examine the coach's boundaries, try to break it, and adapt it to their own courses. Proposed follow-up includes a Day 30 artifact and short monthly check-ins.

I also drafted a policy memo for my dean using TeachAI's seven principles: Purpose, Compliance, Knowledge, Balance, Integrity, Agency, and Evaluation. It asks for institutional guidance on student-facing AI while preserving faculty judgment and a route that does not require AI.

Proposed timeline

  1. Fall 2026–January 2027: Retest v2.2, prepare formal pilot materials, and seek the relevant reviews and approvals.
  2. Spring 2027, Test 1: Run a formal post-test pilot in one section and review the workload, safeguards, and student work.
  3. Spring 2027, Tests 2–4: Consider expansion within my courses if the first phase supports it.
  4. Fall 2027: Offer the workshop to interested colleagues and review their adaptations.
  5. Spring 2028: Explore a coordinated-course implementation with the course's faculty.
  6. 2028, if approved and funded: Explore an institutionally supported coach that could give students access to the same model.

These are proposed dates and decision points. Each phase depends on the evidence and support needed to proceed.

What this evidence does—and does not—show

Testing identified failures and informed revisions to earlier cards. It does not establish that v2.2 works reliably or improves learning. As of October 6, 2026, students are using an early version, but I have no student-outcome data. The comparison study is proposed, and the leadership plan has not been carried out.

The visual model

Follow the student's work.

Read the visual model's detailed text description

The process begins when I return a graded test and share the coach card and assessment expectations. The student selects a missed problem and chooses either the AI coach or a meeting with me. Both routes use the six steps described above.

The student explains the original work, locates the break in reasoning, justifies an error category, repairs each line, writes a cue and action for next time, and develops a new problem using the same idea. Help increases only after an attempt. A student who remains stuck is referred to me.

The student then submits an Error Analysis and a personal reflection. Chat users also submit screenshots. I review the reasoning and record error types. Planned evidence includes the chosen route, requests for answers, agreement between error classifications, the student's next-time plan, transfer contexts, and repeat errors on later tests.

The UDL markers identify representation through symbols or words, engagement through personally relevant problems, and action and expression through the choice of a chat or meeting. Intended outcomes include fewer repeated mistakes and requests for answers, error labels that agree with mine on at least four of five problems, more specific plans, and similar results on both routes. These are goals, not measured results.
