The design challenge

Students often get a graded test back, notice what they missed, and move on without examining the reasoning behind the mistake. I wanted them to identify where their thinking broke, explain the cause, and decide what to do differently next time.

The Error Coach extends the questioning I would use in a meeting while keeping assessment and instructional judgment with me.

How it works

A student chooses one problem from a graded, returned test. They can paste prompt card v2.2 into a new free ChatGPT conversation or work through the same six steps with me in a short meeting:

  1. Explain the original thinking. Describe what they were trying to do.
  2. Find where it broke. Identify the first break in the reasoning.
  3. Name the error type. Choose a category and explain why it fits.
  4. Repair the reasoning. Rework the problem and justify each changed line.
  5. Write a cue for next time. Connect a recognizable cue to a useful action.
  6. Transfer the idea. Build a problem from their own life, or change one feature of the original when a real-life setting does not fit.

The card instructs the coach to ask questions without grading, solving, giving or confirming answers, or repeating identifiable information. Hints come in stages, after an attempt. If a student stays stuck, the coach is instructed to stop and flag the problem for me.

The meeting route is an equal choice, using the same steps and help ladder. Every student writes their own Error Analysis. I assess their reasoning with a 10-point rubric in Appendix C; the coach does not assign the grade.

The visual model

I return a graded test and share the prompt card and assessment expectations. The student selects one missed problem and chooses an AI conversation or a meeting with me. Both routes use the six steps above, with help increasing only after an attempt. A student who remains stuck is referred to me.

The student submits an independently written Error Analysis and reflection. Chat users also submit screenshots; meeting users submit their six-step meeting sheet. I review the reasoning and record error types. Screenshots document the conversation and card version but cannot establish how independently a student worked.

Universal Design for Learning appears through choices of symbols or words, personally relevant problems, and a chat or meeting. Planned evidence includes the chosen route, requests for answers, agreement between error classifications, next-time plans, transfer contexts, and repeated errors on later tests.

Intended outcomes include fewer repeated mistakes and requests for answers, error labels that agree with mine on at least four of five problems, more specific plans, and similar results on both routes. These are goals, not measured results.

Why this design

Ormrod’s (2011) behaviorist principles shape the routine: connect feedback to the student’s response, offer help after an attempt, treat errors as information about their cause, and develop cues for next time. Universal Design for Learning adds choices in how students engage and how mathematics is presented.

Keeping the task to one problem makes the reflection manageable. Requiring the student’s own explanation keeps the emphasis on reasoning.

Testing and the equity audit

I tested v2.0 in eight rounds using the DOMS rubric, then ran twelve simulated equity-audit sessions with v2.1. I conducted those interactions myself; they were not a study with twelve students.

The coach treated names, Spanish, and casual writing with respect in the tested interactions. Three concerns shaped the next revision:

  • Pressure changed the help. Pushing harder could produce stronger hints or reopen a session that should have stopped.
  • Model access mattered. The free model provided weaker coaching than the paid model in my comparison.
  • Notation was lost in audio. Screen readers skipped or garbled parts of the mathematics; useful plain-text explanations came only after a request.

Current v2.2 describes coaching moves instead of scripts to copy, ties help to attempts rather than pressure, and asks every student how they want mathematics presented. These revisions still need testing.

What this evidence does—and does not—show

The tests identified failures and informed revisions. They do not establish that v2.2 works reliably or improves learning. As of October 6, 2026, students are using an early version, but I have no student-outcome data. The comparison study is proposed.

The leadership plan

I designed a 60-minute workshop using UNESCO’s AI Competency Framework for Teachers and drafted a policy memo for my dean using TeachAI’s seven principles. The workshop invites colleagues to examine the coach’s boundaries, test its behavior, and consider adaptations for their own courses.

A proposed timeline moves from retesting and a formal pilot to possible use by colleagues and coordinated courses. Each phase depends on evidence, review, and support; the leadership plan has not been carried out.
